Research

Stellar characterization

Most directly imaged planet host stars are poorly characterized in terms of their abundances, primarily due to their very high rotation velocities (\(v\sin{i}>10~\rm km/s\)), and some even in excess of \(100~\rm km/s\). Such high velocities can cause significant rotational broadening in these stars and lead to blending of adjacent spectral absorption lines, making spectral characterizationa challenging. However, decoding the formation and evolution pathways of these directly imaged planets requires abundance measurements for both the planets and their host stars. A slew of ongoing projects involving both ground-based and space-based instruments have led to measurements of elemental abundance ratios, primarily the carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) ratio , for the companions. My work aims to measure the abundance of 16 elements (C, O, Na, Mg, Si, S, K, Ca, Sc, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Zn, Y), as well as certain abundance ratios (C/O, but also carbon-to-sulfur: C/S and oxygen-to-sulfur: O/S ratios) for directly imaged planet host stars.

I have led a survey on the detailed characterization of these stars using Lick/APF-Levy and Gemini-South/GHOST, with the results published across three first-author papers (Baburaj et al. 2025, 2026a, 2026c, AJ). The fastest rotator I have been able to analyze using these methods is the multi-planetary system host star \(\beta\) Pictoris (\(v\sin{i}\sim120~\rm km/s\)), 60 times faster than our Sun (\(v\sin{i}\sim2~\rm km/s\)). Other fast-rotating imaged planet hosts I have characterized using these methods include HR 8799, 51 Eridani, AF Leporis, and YSES 1. My work shows that assumption of solar abundances for the hosts while benchmarking planetary abundances can lead to incorrect formation inferences.

Exoplanet characterization

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has opened up new frontiers in exoplanet characterization with its excellent data quality allowing us to identify previous undetected spectral features in exoplanet atmospheres in the 3-5 micron range. The most significant was the first detection of \(\rm SO_2\) in the atmosphere of an exoplanet: Alderson et al. (2022) detected \(\rm SO_2\) in the atmosphere of the hot Jupiter WASP-39b using the NIRSpec instrument onboard JWST. Identifying signatures of molecules like sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and ammonia in the atmospheres of exoplanets might allow us access to elemental ratios like C/S, O/S, and N/O, which in addition to C/O, could act as formation tracers, allowing us to decode planet formation. In addition, spectroscopic studies of giant exoplanets also provide insights into the interesting atmospheric physics and chemistry prevailing in their interiors.

In my recent work (Baburaj et al. 2026b, AJ), I obtained the first-ever spectrum of cold GJ 504 b (Teff ~ 550 K), a planetary mass companion (PMC) too faint for ground-based spectroscopy. My work obtains comprehensive R~2,700 3–5-micron spectra of this cold object. Leveraging NIRSpec cross-correlation techniques, I detect the companion at S/N > 300. I also present the first successful PSF subtraction with angular differential imaging (ADI) in the high-contrast regime, detecting GJ 504 b at S/N > 10 in both NRS1 and NRS2, and reaching contrasts < \(10^{-4}\). This enables recovery of the spectral continuum for GJ 504 b, which is lost with the aforementioned cross-correlation techniques. CCF analysis leads to detections of trace species \(\rm CO_2, NH_3, ^{13}CO, C^{18}O,\) and \(\rm H_2S\). Joint retrieval modeling of the JWST spectrum and previous photometry indicates Teff~564 K, [M/H]~0.67, and existence of disequilibrium chemistry. My efforts also provide strong evidence for salt clouds (chlorides/sulfides) in GJ 504 b. Comparison of abundances of GJ 504 b to the primary reveals stellar sulfur, super-stellar carbon, and possibly, oxygen, with the observed metal enrichment only tentatively supporting planet-like formation. My work highlighted the importance of clouds while modeling the spectrum of cold objects (Teff < 600 K). Furthermore, it demonstrates an imminent need for self-consistent atmospheric grids and evolutionary models accounting for clouds, disequilibrium chemistry, and super-solar metallicities in cold objects.​ This work had a press release by Northwestern University (Link to article).

As a graduate student, I sucessfully conceived and executed a Cycle 3 JWST program (GO 5485; PI Baburaj) to observe the \(\sim20~M_{\rm Jup}\) companion HD 206893 B using the NIRSpec IFU. Measuring the detailed abundances of this companion will provide a window into the utility of formation diagnostic tools at the extreme upper end of the planet formation process. I successfully detected HD 206893 B at an angular separation \(\sim0^{\prime\prime}.19~\) despite the bright star saturating the detector at the companion position . Combined atmospheric modeling of the NIRSpec data and previous low-resolution data and photometry is ongoing (Baburaj et al., in prep).

I am the PI of an approved ~13 hr Cycle 5 JWST program (GO 10963) for direct spectroscopy of the recently discovered circumbinary planet HD 143811 AB b (Jones et al. 2025, ApJL), aiming to eluciate differences in planet formation between single-star and binary-star environments. The observations are scheduled for July 2027.

Population studies

Host star abundances provide a glimpse into the elements (and their abundances) that were available for planet formation around them, which serves as a baseline for comparison with the planetary abundances.. Past work has found relationships between planet occurrence and the abundance of individual elements such as Mg, Si, and Ni for the transiting planets discovered by Kepler. However, no such study has been conducted among the directly imaged planet population. Measuring the detailed chemical makeup of the host star (in addition to carbon and oxygen) could allow us to investigate some of these population trends among this population of companions. My past work (Baburaj et al. 2026a, AJ) has suggested that wide-orbit planet host stars could have super-solar C, O abundances. This potential enrichment in 'volatile' elements is the first empirical evidence that the formation environment around wide-orbit companion host stars is potentially different from other planet host stars.

On the companion front, I am interested in performing a population-level analysis of the C/S and O/S ratios as formation tracers and compare them to similar tracers measured for the transiting planet population. It would be interesting if these ratios clearly segregate the two populations of planets like was seen for the C/O ratio (Hoch et al., 2023, AJ).
